The problem

Three things go wrong in the same ten minutes of an evening

01

The kid gets stuck, and the fastest escape is a chatbot that hands over a finished answer. They stop being stuck, and they stop learning.

02

The kid doesn't ask for help, because asking feels like admitting you're bad at it. So they stall, guess, or quit — and nobody finds out until the test.

03

The parent isn't there — other room, other shift, other country — or is there but doesn't know how to help, so they say the answer, or they say "concentrate."

AI made this worse, not better. Nothing on the market makes the slow path — the one where the kid actually learns — easy enough to choose at 9pm.

And in Berlin there's a fourth: a parent whose German isn't fluent can't engage with a German worksheet at all. Their child does homework alone, in a language the household doesn't share. That is not an edge case in this city.

The bet

Why a watch

Kids have one

Most 8–13 year olds here have a kids' smartwatch and don't have a phone. Parents chose that on purpose. It's the only device that's both on the child's wrist all day and trusted by the parent.

The constraint is the thesis

A watch shows one thing at a time, can't be scrolled, can't hold a chatbot. Usually that's a limitation. Here: the problem isn't that kids get too little from AI — it's that they get too much, too fast, too finished. A screen that can only give one hint is a feature.

Nobody else will

Every other team today is building a web app for a laptop. We should say the word "watch" out loud in the first ten seconds of the pitch.

Where Claude sits

Three jobs, none of them "answer the question"

01 · the brake

One hint, never the answer

Sized to this exact problem, citing its actual numbers. Still stuck? A heavier hint. Still stuck? It hands over to a human. The answer is never on the watch.

02 · the translator

Both directions

The stuck moment becomes a coaching card on the parent's phone. A parent's goal, in their own words, becomes one small task the watch can hold.

03 · the observer

Patterns, not scores

"He gives up on word problems after 4 minutes but stays 12 with geometry — he can't parse what the question asks." Then one thing to do tonight.

The two decisions worth defending

What makes it ours

The persistence ring rewards time, not correctness

It fills while the child stays with a problem. Every other system in a child's life rewards the right answer. We reward not quitting — which is the thing that actually transfers.

The third dot is a parent's face

The ladder is visible before they ask: help exists, it's finite, and the last rung is a person. Help shame is the real reason kids don't ask, and three dots on a wrist fix more of it than any amount of encouragement.

The answer is collapsed — from the parent

Two clicks and a "Sure? Try the questions first." We deliberately make it hard for the parent to cheat. Every parent in the room knows they'd click it.

Points buy time with a parent, not toys

"20 minutes of football with Dad." We refuse to teach a child that effort converts into stuff.

The question we will be asked

Why the call isn't a gimmick

A video call is the kind of thing a hackathon team bolts on to look impressive. Ours is the last rung of the ladder. The whole argument is that AI should know when to stop and fetch a human — so it ends at a parent's face, and the parent arrives already briefed, card in hand, three questions ready, answer hidden.

Every other tool here is trying to replace a person.
Ours is engineered to summon one — and to make them better when they arrive.

Say this before you're asked

Where we draw the privacy line

We collectWe never collect
Which problem, how long they stayed, which hints they used, their own mood taps Location. Audio. Camera. Messages. Screen time. Anything outside a homework session.

Learning behaviour only

The tool points at a worksheet, never at a child. We don't read their AI chats.

The child can see the deal

There's a screen on the child's watch listing exactly what's shared, in words a ten-year-old reads. Open the Me tab above. Nobody else will have this.

Guidance, never judgement

"Do this tonight," never "your child has an attention problem." The prompt forbids the words lazy, distracted, behind, struggling, weak.
