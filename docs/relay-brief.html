The brief says: help real people use AI

So a parent has to be the one using it

An app that quietly runs analysis in the background is not a person using AI — it's a feature. The judges will say so.

Here the parent types the question themselves, in their own words, the way they'd say it to a friend. What makes it work isn't a clever interface — it's that for the first time they have something worth asking about: their own child's real data, and their own worry. Most parents have never had a reason to open an AI. This is one.

And they learn to use it better

Every answer ends with what made that question answerable — "you gave me five days of times instead of saying he's lazy." They take that to any AI, anywhere. That's the learn half of the brief, and it costs us one field in the response.

Starter questions are the on-ramp

A parent who has never used AI doesn't know what to type. Four real questions sit on the screen as chips. Blank-page paralysis is the actual barrier, not scepticism.

The problem

Parents can see the symptom and never the cause

01

What a parent sees: homework starting at half nine, again. Bedtime creeping. A kid who seems slower than last year.

02

What they conclude: he's getting lazy. Because that's the only explanation visible from the kitchen at 21:40.

03

What's actually happening: two days a week he gets home ninety minutes later. Nobody has ever put those two columns side by side — including the parent, who has all the information and none of the pattern.

And the family that needs this most is the one where the parent works a late shift, or reads German slowly, or simply isn't in the room at 17:00. They aren't absent. They're missing one column.

The bet

Why a watch

Kids have one

Most 8–13 year olds here have a kids' smartwatch and don't have a phone. Parents chose that on purpose. It's the only device that's on the child's wrist all day and trusted by the parent.

A tap is the only honest input

No child fills in a form. No child answers "how was your day" usefully. One tap at the moment it happens is the only way to get a timestamp that's true — and timestamps are the entire raw material of the product.

Nobody else will build for it

Every other team today is building a web app for a laptop. Say the word "watch" in the first ten seconds of the pitch.

What's on each device

A rich watch, a thinking phone

the watch · the child's

Eight taps, one day

Woke, left, got home, moved, started homework, read, screens off, in bed. Each tap is a timestamp and some points. A ring fills as the day takes shape — it shows the shape, it doesn't grade it.

the watch · the child's

Points buy time, not toys

"20 minutes of football with Dad." "A whole afternoon, just you and Mum." We refuse to teach a child that effort converts into stuff. Plus a one-tap mood, and a streak they care about more than we expect.

the phone · the parent's

Ask anything, in your own words

Claude answers from the real times, quotes them, separates behaviour from cause, and says "I can't see that from this" when it can't. Then: one thing to do tonight.

the phone · the parent's

Guidance that reaches the child

The advice becomes a short message in the parent's own voice on the watch — never a reminder, never a reprimand. Or one tap to call. The AI stops at the doorway; a parent walks through it.

Say this before you're asked

This is the one that can sink us

We are building behaviour tracking for children. If we don't draw the line out loud, a judge will draw it for us.

We collectWe never collect
The times the child tapped, which ones they skipped, the face they picked, points and streak Location. Audio. Camera. Messages. Screen time. Contacts. Anything the child didn't tap themselves.

The child taps it, or it doesn't exist

Every data point in the system was put there by the child, on purpose. Nothing is sensed, inferred or collected in the background. That one rule answers most of the question.

The child can see the deal

Open the Me tab on the watch above. A complete list of what's shared, in words a ten-year-old reads. Nobody else in the room will have this.

Guidance, never judgement

The prompt forbids the words lazy, distracted, behind, struggling, weak, and every clinical label. Output is "do this tonight", never "your child has a problem."

The two minutes, shot by shot

0:00A parent on our team, no slides: "My son starts his homework at half nine. I've been telling him he's lazy for a month."
0:15Watch: he taps Got home. 18:51. The ring fills a notch. "One tap. That's the whole input."
0:30Phone. She types it the way she'd say it: "Is he getting slower, or is something else going on?"
0:45The answer, with her own numbers in it: "Three days he's home by 17:10 and starts at 18:25. Two days he's home at 18:50 and starts at 21:40. Same boy, ninety minutes less day." Let that sit.
1:05Tonight: "Don't mention homework. Ask him what Tuesdays actually look like now."
1:20She taps send to his watch. It arrives in her voice, not the app's: "Tuesdays look long from where I'm sitting — want to tell me about them tonight?" He taps OK.
1:35Scroll to the skill card: "You gave me five days of times instead of saying he's lazy. That's what made this answerable." Say: "She just learned to use AI. We didn't teach her — the answer did."
1:50The child's Me screen. "He tapped every one of these himself. We sense nothing." Repo link. Stop.

No slides. No architecture diagram. No code on screen. A real parent's voice, two real devices.
