The brief says: help real people use AI

So we gave an AI to both of them

The parent asks about her own child

She types it the way she'd say it to a friend. What makes it work isn't the interface — it's that for the first time she has something worth asking about: her own child's real data, and her own worry. Most parents have never had a reason to open an AI. This is one.

The child asks about their own evening

"I've got football and homework — which first?" The watch helps him order his own evening instead of doing anything for him. A ten-year-old learning that an AI is a thinking tool, not an answer machine, is the single most durable thing we could teach today.

And both of them learn to ask better

Every answer to the parent ends with what made that question answerable — "you gave me five days of times instead of saying he's lazy." That's the learn half of the brief, and it costs one field in the response.

Starter questions are the on-ramp

Someone who has never used AI doesn't know what to type. Real questions sit on both screens as chips. Blank-page paralysis is the actual barrier, not scepticism.

The problem

Parents can see the symptom and never the cause

01

What a parent sees: homework starting at half nine, again. Bedtime creeping. A kid who seems slower than last year.

02

What they conclude: he's getting lazy. It's the only explanation visible from the kitchen at 21:40.

03

What's actually happening: two days a week he gets home ninety minutes later, because training moved across town. Nobody ever put those two columns side by side — including the parent, who has all the information and none of the pattern.

And then you see why

Pins, not a trail

The map shows only the places a check-in happened — he tapped "got home", so home gets a pin. There is no background location, no breadcrumb, no "where is he right now". The rule stays exactly what it was: the child taps it, or it doesn't exist.

The map is the punchline, not a feature

The chart shows that Tuesdays are different. The map shows why in one glance: training moved to a hall across town. A parent who sees this stops saying "hurry up" and starts asking about Tuesdays.

What's on each device

A rich watch, a serious phone app

watch · the child's

Eight taps, one day

Woke, left, got home, moved, homework, read, screens off, in bed. Each tap is a timestamp, a place and some points. A ring fills as the day takes shape — it shows the shape, it doesn't grade it.

watch · the child's

His own AI

Plan tonight, decide what to drop, ask why he keeps running out of evening. Short answers, watch-sized, spoken aloud. It never does the thing for him.

watch · the child's

Points buy time, not toys

"20 minutes of football with Dad." We refuse to teach a child that effort converts into stuff. Plus a one-tap mood and a streak he cares about more than we expect.

phone · the parent's

Ask anything

Claude answers from the real times, quotes them, separates behaviour from cause, and says "I can't see that from this" when it can't.

phone · the parent's

Week, map, history

The chart, the pins, and every conversation she's had before — fourteen weeks of them, searchable. This isn't a session; it's a record of a childhood she can scroll back through.

phone · the parent's

Video or audio, one tap

Video when she's home, audio when she's on the tram. The chart stays on screen while they talk. The AI stops at the doorway; a parent walks through it.

The bet

Why a watch

Kids have one

Most 8–13 year olds here have a kids' smartwatch and don't have a phone. Parents chose that on purpose. It's the only device that's on the child's wrist all day and trusted by the parent.

A tap is the only honest input

No child fills in a form. No child answers "how was your day" usefully. One tap at the moment it happens is the only way to get a timestamp that's true — and timestamps are the entire raw material.

Nobody else will build for it

Every other team today is building a web app for a laptop. Say the word "watch" in the first ten seconds of the pitch.

Say this before you're asked

This is the one that can sink us

We are building behaviour tracking for children, and now there's a map in it. If we don't draw the line out loud, a judge will draw it for us.

We collectWe never collect
The times he tapped, which ones he skipped, the place attached to a tap he made, the face he picked, points and streak Continuous location. Any location he didn't attach himself. Audio. Camera. Messages. Screen time. Contacts. Anything outside a check-in.

He taps it, or it doesn't exist

Every point in the system was put there by the child, on purpose — the places included. Nothing is sensed or inferred in the background. That one rule answers most of the question, including the map.

He can see the deal

Open the Me tab on the watch above. A complete list of what's shared, in words a ten-year-old reads, and the map is on it. Nobody else in the room will have this.

Guidance, never judgement

The prompt forbids lazy, distracted, behind, struggling, weak, and every clinical label. Output is "do this tonight", never "your child has a problem."

No live location, ever

There is deliberately no "where is he now". That is the product a dozen companies already sell, and it's the one that makes a child feel followed. We are not building it, and we will say so.
